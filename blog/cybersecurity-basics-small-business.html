Why Small Businesses Get Hacked (And 3 Things That Actually Stop It)

Security Small Business Best Practices
Cybersecurity Basics
Share:

"We're too small to be a target" is one of the most common — and most costly — assumptions a business owner can make. In reality, small businesses are attractive targets, precisely because their defenses tend to be thin: no dedicated IT staff, no security budget, and systems that were set up once and never revisited.

The good news: most breaches don't involve sophisticated hacking. They exploit basic, well-known gaps — the kind that take an afternoon to close, not a security team.

The Myth of "We're Too Small to Notice"

Attackers rarely pick targets one at a time. Most attacks are automated — scripts that scan the internet for outdated software, exposed logins, or leaked passwords, then strike wherever they find an opening. Company size doesn't matter to a script. What matters is whether the door was left unlocked.

Where Most Breaches Actually Start

Very few incidents begin with a mysterious zero-day exploit. In practice, the same handful of causes show up again and again:

  • Reused passwords. One leaked password from an unrelated site gets tried everywhere else.
  • No multi-factor authentication (MFA). A password alone is rarely enough to stop account takeover.
  • Outdated software and plugins. Known vulnerabilities that were patched months ago, but never applied.
  • Phishing emails. A convincing message that gets someone to click, or hand over credentials.

None of these require an advanced attacker — just an unmaintained system and a little patience.

Three Things That Actually Stop Most Attacks

1. Use a password manager, and never reuse a password

A password manager generates and stores a unique, strong password for every account, so a leak on one site can't be used to break into another. This alone closes one of the most common attack paths — and it takes minutes to set up.

2. Turn on multi-factor authentication everywhere it's offered

MFA means a stolen password isn't enough on its own. Prioritize email first — it's usually the key to resetting everything else — then banking, hosting, and any admin panel your business depends on.

3. Keep software updated, and remove what you no longer use

Outdated plugins, themes, and dependencies are one of the most common entry points for automated attacks. If a tool, integration, or account isn't in active use anymore, removing it removes a door that no one's watching.

What This Looks Like Day-to-Day

None of this requires a security background. It's closer to routine maintenance: a password manager for the team, MFA switched on across the accounts that matter most, and a periodic check for anything running outdated software. Most of it is a one-time setup, not an ongoing burden.
